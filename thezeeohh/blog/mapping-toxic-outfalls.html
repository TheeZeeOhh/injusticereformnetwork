Mapping Toxic Outfalls: A Guide to Citizen Air Quality Audits

By Dr. Keisha Morgan • June 18, 2026 • 9 min read

Communities living next to industrial facilities are often exposed to dangerous levels of air pollution while regulators look the other way. This guide equips you with the tools to document violations yourself — using low-cost technology to build evidence strong enough for Title VI civil rights complaints, EPA complaints, and community campaigns.

Real Impact: Richmond, California’s Citizen Air Monitoring Network

In Richmond, CA, near the Chevron refinery, community groups like Brightline Defense deployed low-cost Clarity and PurpleAir sensors in fenceline neighborhoods. The data revealed hyperlocal pollution hotspots that official monitors missed. This citizen-generated evidence supported campaigns for stronger enforcement, public health interventions during wildfires, and policy changes under California’s AB 617 program. Their work became a blueprint for other communities fighting industrial pollution.

Why Citizen Air Quality Audits Work

Agencies often lack real-time data near fenceline communities. Your organized, methodical monitoring can fill that gap and create legally viable records that force accountability.

Step-by-Step: How to Run a Citizen Air Quality Audit

1. Preparation & Safety

  • Form a small team (3–5 people) for safety and consistency.
  • Review the facility’s permit via ECHO.EPA.gov.
  • Identify wind patterns and likely outfall points.
  • Purchase or borrow low-cost monitors: PurpleAir, AirGradient, or Clarity (~$150–$300).

2. Deploy the Monitors

  • Place sensors at multiple points along the fenceline (public property or with permission).
  • Log GPS coordinates, time, weather conditions, and wind direction for every reading.
  • Run monitors for at least 2–4 weeks for credible baseline data.

3. Document Everything

  • Use a standardized log sheet (download template below).
  • Take timestamped photos and videos of visible emissions.
  • Export data from the sensor dashboard regularly.

4. Turn Data Into Action

  • Compare readings against EPA standards and the facility’s permit limits.
  • File a Title VI complaint with the EPA Office of Civil Rights if the pollution disproportionately impacts communities of color.
  • Present findings at city council meetings and to local media.
